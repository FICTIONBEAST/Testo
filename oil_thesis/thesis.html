Where we are

Geopolitical

US–Israeli strikes on Iran began in late February. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed for about 204 days; an April ceasefire and the 17 June memorandum (60 days of toll-free passage, demining within 30 days) both collapsed, the US blockade of Iranian exports was reinstated on 14 July and Kharg loadings stopped on 31 July. This month's ladder: the US sank eight Iranian tankers, Iran struck ten ships, two US vessels and a base in Jordan and declared a "restricted zone" into the Gulf of Oman; drones from Iraq hit the Saudi East–West pipeline in at least eight places on 10–11 September and Riyadh shut it; Houthis struck Yanbu twice and fired the first ballistic missile at Riyadh on 19 September. The GCC–Iran meeting in Salalah was postponed indefinitely; Qatar is shuttling proposals during UN General Assembly week; Trump meets Gulf leaders on 22 September and Xi on 24 September with secondary sanctions on Iran's buyers on the table. Prediction markets put Hormuz traffic returning to normal by 30 September at about 2 %.

Economic

Headline CPI 3.4 % with energy at +14.7 %, core 2.5 %, unemployment 4.1 %. The Fed hiked 25 bp on 16 September to 3.75–4.00 % by a 12–0 vote, the first hike since 2023, and 16 of 18 participants project another. The dollar index crossed 100. IEA sees 2026 demand down 2.5 mb/d (Q3 −3.4, Q4 −2.0) with the losses in Asian middle distillates; OPEC still models +0.4 mb/d, a divergence nobody has resolved. Equities sit 2.7 % below their August high. EIA's outlook has Brent averaging ~$90 in 2H26; the post-June bank calls ($75–86) predate the July breakdown and the Saudi damage.

Physical

World supply 100.1 mb/d in August, down 5.7 mb/d year on year with more than 10 mb/d of Gulf output shut in and full Middle East recovery deferred to 2027. Saudi output fell to 6.24 mb/d; Riyadh is now exporting under US naval escort along the Omani coast (about 6 mb on one recent day) and expects the East–West line back at 40–60 % within roughly two weeks. Global inventories are down 400 mb year to date; US refineries run at 97.8 %; the diesel crack printed a record $106 on 1 September; VLCC earnings are at a record ~$800k/day and war-risk cover is 40× pre-crisis. From Q3 to Q4 demand recovers ~1.4 mb/d while supply is flat: a 1–1.5 mb/d deficit drawn from depleted stocks in the heating season.

Why long, and why not short, now

  1. Supply loss still exceeds demand loss. −5.7 mb/d of 2026 supply against −2.5 mb/d of demand, and the seasonal demand recovery arrives before any Gulf supply recovery.
  2. The buffers that broke the March–June spike are gone. March had a 400 mb coordinated release and normal product stocks. Today the SPR is at 308 mb, distillate is at multi-decade lows and refineries have no slack. The next hit meets a market with no cushion; convexity is to the upside.
  3. The calendar defers the bearish catalyst. Washington says no relief before 3 November; Tehran says no reopening before its conditions are met. The pre-election tail, a "big decision" on a major strike, points up.
  4. Carry favours the long. At $4.09 of prompt backwardation a short pays ~4 % a month to wait for a catalyst scheduled for November; a long is paid the same to be wrong on timing.
  5. The back already prices normalisation. Dec-27 at $79.03 is below EIA's $90 and near the old bank forecasts. A short in the back has little to earn; a short in the front is a bet on a pre-election deal.
  6. Positioning is not crowded. Managed-money Brent length (~265k lots) is below its May peak and funds sold products into 15 September.

The four-day slide from $108 to $101 was headline-driven: Qatar-mediated messages, UNGA meetings, escorted Saudi transits, a pipeline restart "soon". None of that changes the balance before November. The June template (−44 % in nine weeks) needed a signed memorandum, a fuller SPR and normal product stocks; a repeat from here would find a higher floor.

The short thesis, and when it becomes the trade

The bear case is the same facts on a different clock: demand destruction compounds (China −8.9 %, the Fed hiking into an energy shock, the dollar above 100); the end of the war is being telegraphed by the one actor who can lift the blockade and markets discount known events early; Saudi barrels are leaking out under escort and Iran is admitting "friendly" tonnage; a US product-export restriction would crush NY Harbor cracks; speculative length gives back 4–6 % on talk alone.

When: after 3 November as the base case; earlier the moment three bearish triggers fire; immediately if a decisive US strike is read as war-ending, because that is the June pattern in fast-forward. How: the curve rather than flat price. Short Mar-27 / long Dec-27 Brent at ~$10.40, target $3, stop $16: a deal collapses backwardation while the back barely moves; escalation widens it. Outright, a Mar-27 $85/$70 put spread; never naked short futures into an OVX-50 market with a $30 upside tail. Target after a deal: $80–85, not $70: mine clearance takes 40–50 days (the Pentagon says up to six months), insurance is quick up and slow down, the SPR must be refilled, Saudi infrastructure is damaged, and distillate demand peaks in Q1.

What would make this wrong

  • The timetable moves forward. If Qatar lands a framework during UNGA week, scenario A arrives inside the horizon and the long loses its $15 fast. That is why entry is scaled and the loss defined.
  • Demand falls faster than supply. A Fed hiking into 3.4 % inflation is the 2022 template, when oil fell from $120 to $80 in six months despite a war. The physical loss is far larger now and there is no SPR to lean on, but products are unforgiving. Trigger B7.
  • Escorted flows scale quietly. If protected transits reach 20–30 a day and Iran declines to attack them, the premium erodes without a headline. Trigger B2.
  • The June memory beats the facts. Every rally now meets sellers at $105–110; profit-taking is staged for that reason.
  • Weather. A strong El Niño points to a milder northern-tier winter, blunting the distillate kicker; NOAA's mid-October outlook is the check.
  • Model risk. The probabilities are judgment from a single crisis year. Risk control is structural (stop, put strike, time stop), not statistical.
